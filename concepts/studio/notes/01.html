Ask a simple question inside almost any organization: how much did we do with Harbor Supply last year? Finance pulls a number from the ledger. Sales pulls a different one from the CRM. Someone in operations remembers a third figure from an email thread. All three people are right, and all three numbers are wrong.

The problem isn't the math. It's that "Harbor Supply" isn't one thing in your systems. It's five.

How one company becomes five

In the ledger it's vendor 10423, set up years ago by someone who has since left. In the CRM it's "Harbor Supply Co." with a period, because that's how a salesperson typed it. In the inbox it's "HSC," because that's what their team signs their emails with. The payment processor knows it by a customer ID. And the spreadsheet your controller actually trusts calls it "Harbor (old account)" because of a merger nobody fully cleaned up.

No single record is a mistake. Each system named the company the way that system needed to. But nothing connects them, so every question that crosses systems starts with a person doing detective work.

The fix isn't renaming everything. It's giving every name somewhere to point.

What it costs

The cost rarely shows up as a line item. It shows up as the Monday meeting that spends twenty minutes reconciling two reports. It shows up as a price increase sent to a customer who was already on a special agreement under a different account. It shows up when the one person who knows that "HSC" and vendor 10423 are the same company goes on vacation.

Giving every name somewhere to point

The answer isn't a massive cleanup project where everyone agrees on one true name. Those projects stall, because every system still needs its own. The answer is a layer above the systems that knows these five records are one company, keeps each source's name as an alias, and remembers where each fact came from.

Once that exists, the question "how much did we do with Harbor Supply?" has one answer, and you can see exactly which records it was built from.
