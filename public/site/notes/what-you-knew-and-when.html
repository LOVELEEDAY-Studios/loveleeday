What you knew, and when you knew it.

Most systems overwrite yesterday's value with today's. That keeps the present tidy and makes every past decision impossible to judge fairly.

In March, a team decided to keep a supplier for another year. Six months later, that decision looks like a mistake. Deliveries slipped, orders went out late, and someone asks the obvious question in a review: why did we do that?

The honest answer is usually "because of what we knew in March." But that answer is almost impossible to prove, because the system that held the March numbers has been updated every day since. The record doesn't show what was known then. It only shows what is known now.

Two different kinds of time

Every fact in an organization has two dates attached to it, whether the system keeps them or not. There is the date something was true in the world: the supplier's delivery terms changed on May 1. And there is the date the organization found out: the notice of the change arrived on June 12.

Most systems keep neither. They keep the current value, and when it changes, the old one is overwritten. That works well for running the business today. It works badly for understanding any decision made before today, because the evidence the decision rested on is gone.

Judging the past with the future

When the history is gone, reviews fall into a quiet trap. People judge a March decision using today's numbers, because today's numbers are the only ones left. The decision looks careless, when it was actually reasonable with what was on the table. The person who made it can't defend it, and the lesson the organization takes away is the wrong one.

It cuts the other way too. A decision that turned out well by luck gets credited as good judgment, and the same reasoning gets repeated where it doesn't hold.

A fair review asks what was known at the time, not what is known now.

Add, don't overwrite

The fix is simple to say. When a value changes, keep the old one. Record the new value alongside it, with the date it became true and the date you learned it. Nothing is erased. The present is still easy to read, because the latest value is still the latest. But the past stays intact, and you can ask a system a question it could never answer before: what did we believe on March 10?

That one question changes how an organization learns. Reviews become fair. Patterns in late information become visible, like a supplier whose changes of terms always reach you six weeks after they take effect. And the people who made hard calls with incomplete information get judged on the information they actually had.

This is how memory works in what LOVELEEDAY builds. Arthur keeps what was true and when it became known, so new information adds context without erasing the history a decision was made on.
